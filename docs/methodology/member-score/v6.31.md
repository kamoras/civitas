# Member score v6.31

Corrections to the inputs of Funding Independence and the House's
cosponsorship measures, found by the 2026-10-08 data audit.

## Small-donor share when a campaign itemizes every gift

The small-donor share is the FEC's unitemized individual contributions over
contributions. A campaign that itemizes every gift reports $0 unitemized,
so its share read 0%: one senator in every cycle, and 22 House members in
the stored data (the audit counted 26 over $500K raised, by its own cut).
Their small donors are among the itemized; the filings can't say how much.
The share is now not measured for them (`smallDonorPercentage` null),
left out of Funding Independence like any part that can't be measured,
and left out of the chamber's small-donor reference.

The FEC's contributions-by-size tables were checked as a replacement and
rejected: they total more than the campaigns' own individual
contributions (one Senate campaign's 2020 figures: $69.2M against $59.9M;
another's 2024: $4.25M against $2.70M), consistent with earmarked memo
entries counted twice, and are empty for some candidates.

Estimated before release on that stored data: the senator +6 on Funding
Independence; the 22 House members between −4 and +5 (mean +0.6); every
other member within ±1 (the reference moves slightly). Those counts were
taken on the election windows before v6.30, which moved several members'
funding to the election that seated them; on the data the release
actually scored, one senator and one House member itemize every gift
(see "Measured effect" below).

## Donor lists

- **Joint fundraising committees** (FEC designation "J") are no longer
  donors. What one sends a participant is the participant's share of
  individual gifts, which the candidate reports as a transfer and the FEC's
  totals already leave out; listed, it was the campaign's own fundraising
  counted again.
- **Employer rows are employees' money** by construction. 1,352 Senate
  rows (universities, law firms, technology companies) had taken the name
  classifier's type for the name, mostly "Party/Ideological".

## Industry breakdown summed past the contributions

76 members' industry breakdowns added up to more than their total
contributions. Two causes, both in the committee part (the occupation part
was already capped at the itemized total):

- **The detail read a different window from the totals.** It covered the
  election's full period (six years for a senator) while the FEC's totals
  for that election can start later: a senator first seated by a special
  election has a regular election whose totals begin after it, and a
  first-time candidate's committee totals begin with the campaign. One
  senator's breakdown counted $2.9M of committee money against $1.2M in
  the totals, the difference being the special election's. The detail now
  covers the cycles the totals' own rows cover (the full period only when
  none is held). 31 members' windows change.
- **The bulk file is what the giving committees reported**, and it runs
  over what the campaign reports receiving where the money came through a
  joint fundraising committee, which the campaign files as a transfer and
  the totals (and the shares) leave out. One House leader: $2.07M reported
  given, $187K received as contributions, $12M of transfers. 54 members
  are still over after the window fix. The committee part is scaled to the
  committee money the campaign reported (other political plus party
  committees), keeping its proportions, as the occupation part is scaled to
  the itemized total. The top-donor list keeps each committee's own
  reported figure.

The industry mix's concentration is part of Funding Independence, so these
members' scores move; "Measured effect" below has the numbers.

## PACs with no sponsoring organization

A PAC registered with the FEC with no organization type and no connected
organization (a nonconnected committee) is now POLITICAL by its
registration, as party and leadership committees already were. The name
classifier had been reading them: of a random 80 of the 142 such PACs among
stored donors, judged by hand, 55 are ideological or issue committees, and
it filed two in three of those under an industry (one as real estate on 125
rows, an environmental-justice PAC as firearms, a pro-Israel PAC as private
prisons). The other 25 are partnership PACs (law and accounting firms,
which cannot register a corporate sponsor) and physician groups; under the
rule their money counts toward no industry instead of, mostly, the wrong
one. On the 80: 69% correct against 35% (McNemar, 35 names only the rule
gets right against 8 only the classifier does, p < 0.001). A hybrid that
kept the classifier's industry labels and overrode only its lobbying ones
scored 56%.

Removing organization names from the industry descriptions was measured
too, and not done: on the 659 PACs the FEC and SEC records label, accuracy
fell from 0.455 to 0.408 (p = 0.0004), and on names sharing no word with a
description it was unchanged (0.148 both). Taking them out of the private
prisons description alone cut that label from 17 stored names to 2 but
moved about 45 others through the shared threshold, as many for the worse
as for the better.

## House cosponsorship

Cosponsors were fetched for only the first five sponsored bills of each
House member, and only those bills entered the cosponsorship network: 18%
of the House's 12,287 current-Congress bills. Leadership (PageRank),
bipartisan coalition (which the audit found saturated at 0 or 1 for about
half the House) and the ideology prior read that sliver. Every
current-Congress bill is now read, as in the Senate since 2026-07.
Cosponsor lists are cached a week (they change slowly), so a night
refreshes about 2,700 of the 19,000 lists across both chambers. The first
night after release fetches about 10,000 and runs about two hours longer.

With every bill read, the House network went from 1,581 bills with
cosponsors (17,656 cosponsorships) to 9,598 (142,042). "Measured effect"
below has what that did to the scores.

## Votes: who took a side

- **"Present" is its own answer.** It was stored as "Not Voting", so a
  member who answered present was shown as absent and counted as a missed
  vote in participation. It is now shown as cast, counts as attending, and
  carries no party signal. The stored roll calls hold about 230 present
  answers outside quorum calls and Speaker elections, at most nine for any
  member, so participation moves by about a point at most.
- **A party's lean on a roll call is read from the members who voted Yea
  or Nay**, as in CQ's party unity votes. Absent and present members were
  counted as not voting Yea, so a party's absences could decide the label:
  a 143-73 Republican vote against the Democrats' 0-212 read bipartisan.
  8 of the 1,588 stored roll calls change label (6 Senate, 2 House), and
  with them the voted-with-party flag of the members who voted on them.

## Measured effect

Measured 2026-10-09 by replaying both member pipelines offline on a copy
of the production database taken after that night's run. Every request
was answered from the API responses that run cached, so the code is the
only thing that differs: the replay ran once with the code just before
this release and once with this release's, on the same responses and the
same learning store, and each member's scores were compared. The
release's parts were then separated by rescoring this release's inputs
with one part swapped back to the earlier replay's. Replaying with the
v6.33 code reproduces every score stored that night but one, and that
one is explained below.

| Dimension | Senate (100) | House (433) |
|---|---|---|
| Funding Independence | 75 moved (23 up, 52 down), −7 to +8, mean −0.26 | 183 moved (70 up, 113 down), −3 to +13, mean +0.07 |
| Constituent Alignment | 24 moved (16 up, 8 down), −5 to +10, mean +0.24 | 167 moved (98 up, 69 down), −12 to +13, mean −0.23 |
| Legislative Effectiveness | none | 394 moved (198 up, 196 down), −13 to +25; 10th–90th percentile −7 to +10; mean +0.51 |
| Funding Diversity (informational) | 66 moved, all down, −13 to −1 | 270 moved, all down, −25 to −1 |
| Overall | 75 moved, −2.6 to +3.6, mean −0.01 | 417 moved, −6.7 to +9.5, mean +0.12 |

Of the 533 members, 492 moved overall: 93 by 3 points or more, 20 by 5 or
more, none by 10. The mean overall score rose 0.10. The largest moves on
each dimension: Legislative Effectiveness +25, +22 and +21, and −13 (all
House); Constituent Alignment +13 and three at −12 (House), +10 (Senate);
Funding Independence +13 and +11 (House), +8 and −7 (Senate); Funding
Diversity −25 (House), −13 and −12 (Senate).

By part:

- **House cosponsorship** is all of the Legislative Effectiveness change,
  through leadership (PageRank) and coalition attraction read from the
  whole record: 329 representatives moved by 2 or more, 179 by 5 or more,
  56 by 10 or more. No senator's score moved.
- **Party labels** (the 8 roll calls that changed label, and 3 House roll
  calls left unlabeled once only Yea and Nay count) are all of the
  Constituent Alignment change. They changed the party-line record of 88
  senators and 114 representatives, by one vote or one break each. A
  member near the low end of the break range moves far on one break: the
  House members who moved 9 to 13 points had 2 to 6 breaks in about 295
  party-line votes, and one more or one fewer. Others moved by a point or
  two through the per-run expectation, which is refitted on the whole
  chamber: 69 representatives and one senator moved with an unchanged
  record, none by more than 2. "Present" answers have no further effect, since participation is
  not scored, and the independent's "no party line" status changed no
  score.
- **PACs with no sponsoring organization** counted as political: Funding
  Independence moved for 38 senators (−3 to +3) and 156 representatives
  (−3 to +11), and Funding Diversity fell for 57 senators and 253
  representatives, mostly by 1 or 2 points (at most 5).
- **The other funding corrections** (the industry breakdown's window and
  committee cap, joint fundraising committees, the small-donor share)
  moved Funding Independence for 63 senators (−8 to +8) and 73
  representatives (−2 to +13), and Funding Diversity for 17 senators (down
  to −12) and 37 representatives (down to −25). Taken alone, the donor-list
  changes (joint fundraising committees removed, employer rows typed as
  employees' money) moved 17 senators and 5 representatives by 2 points or
  more (up to +8 and +10); the rest of that part's moves were the chamber
  reference shifting by a point.
- **Small-donor share not measured**: on the data the release scored, it
  applies to one senator (Funding Independence 35 to 40) and one House
  member (47 to 48). Ten other senators and five other representatives
  moved by a point as the chamber reference changed.

These parts overlap (each was measured with the others in place), so
their counts don't sum to the totals above, which are the release's
combined effect.

The senator whose small-donor share is not measured was never stored with
the new figure: the scorecard validator clamped the share to a number, and
a share marked not measured (None) raised, so from the first run of this release that
senator failed every night and kept the record of the night before. The
validator now keeps None. The replay measures the score the release
computes, which is what the next run stores.

## Also in this release (no score effect)

- Partisan depth credits each policy area a bill touches with the bill's
  own lean, from the roll call where there is one, not with each area's
  content label (AGENTS.md §4).
- House sponsored bills show the CRS policy area Congress.gov assigns, as
  the Senate's do, and are classified on the title and that area together.
- A member who caucuses with neither party has the vote-part status
  "no party line", not "few votes".
- Leaderboard trend arrows compare only snapshots that recorded the same
  algorithm version.
- The donor-vote cards headed each industry's total with the industry's
  largest donor, which read as that one donor's money (one education
  total of $460K under a single university's name). The industry is now
  the heading and the largest donor is named on its own line.
- Vote dates are stored as ISO dates, so the voting record lists votes in
  calendar order.
