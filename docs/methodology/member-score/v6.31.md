# Member score v6.31

Corrections to the inputs of Funding Independence and the House's
cosponsorship measures, found by the 2026-10-08 data audit.

## Small-donor share when a campaign itemizes every gift

The small-donor share is the FEC's unitemized individual contributions over
contributions. A campaign that itemizes every gift reports $0 unitemized,
so its share read 0%: one senator in every cycle, and 22 House members in
the stored data (the audit counted 26 over $500K raised, by its own cut).
Their small donors are among the itemized; the filings can't say how much.
The share is now not measured for them (`smallDonorPercentage` null),
left out of Funding Independence like any part that can't be measured,
and left out of the chamber's small-donor reference.

The FEC's contributions-by-size tables were checked as a replacement and
rejected: they total more than the campaigns' own individual
contributions (one Senate campaign's 2020 figures: $69.2M against $59.9M;
another's 2024: $4.25M against $2.70M), consistent with earmarked memo
entries counted twice, and are empty for some candidates.

Effect on the stored data: the senator +6 on Funding Independence; the 22
House members between −4 and +5 (mean +0.6); every other member within
±1 (the reference moves slightly).

## Donor lists

- **Joint fundraising committees** (FEC designation "J") are no longer
  donors. What one sends a participant is the participant's share of
  individual gifts, which the candidate reports as a transfer and the FEC's
  totals already leave out; listed, it was the campaign's own fundraising
  counted again.
- **Employer rows are employees' money** by construction. 1,352 Senate
  rows (universities, law firms, technology companies) had taken the name
  classifier's type for the name, mostly "Party/Ideological".

## Industry breakdown summed past the contributions

76 members' industry breakdowns added up to more than their total
contributions. Two causes, both in the committee part (the occupation part
was already capped at the itemized total):

- **The detail read a different window from the totals.** It covered the
  election's full period (six years for a senator) while the FEC's totals
  for that election can start later: a senator first seated by a special
  election has a regular election whose totals begin after it, and a
  first-time candidate's committee totals begin with the campaign. One
  senator's breakdown counted $2.9M of committee money against $1.2M in
  the totals, the difference being the special election's. The detail now
  covers the cycles the totals' own rows cover (the full period only when
  none is held). 31 members' windows change.
- **The bulk file is what the giving committees reported**, and it runs
  over what the campaign reports receiving where the money came through a
  joint fundraising committee, which the campaign files as a transfer and
  the totals (and the shares) leave out. One House leader: $2.07M reported
  given, $187K received as contributions, $12M of transfers. 54 members
  are still over after the window fix. The committee part is scaled to the
  committee money the campaign reported (other political plus party
  committees), keeping its proportions, as the occupation part is scaled to
  the itemized total. The top-donor list keeps each committee's own
  reported figure.

The industry mix's concentration is part of Funding Independence, so these
members' scores move; the effect is measured on the first run and added
here.

## House cosponsorship

Cosponsors were fetched for only the first five sponsored bills of each
House member, and only those bills entered the cosponsorship network: 18%
of the House's 12,287 current-Congress bills. Leadership (PageRank),
bipartisan coalition (which the audit found saturated at 0 or 1 for about
half the House) and the ideology prior read that sliver. Every
current-Congress bill is now read, as in the Senate since 2026-07.
Cosponsor lists are cached a week (they change slowly), so a night
refreshes about 2,700 of the 19,000 lists across both chambers. The first
night after release fetches about 10,000 and runs about two hours longer.

The effect on scores needs those lists; it is measured on the first run's
data and added here.

## Votes: who took a side

- **"Present" is its own answer.** It was stored as "Not Voting", so a
  member who answered present was shown as absent and counted as a missed
  vote in participation. It is now shown as cast, counts as attending, and
  carries no party signal. The stored roll calls hold about 230 present
  answers outside quorum calls and Speaker elections, at most nine for any
  member, so participation moves by about a point at most.
- **A party's lean on a roll call is read from the members who voted Yea
  or Nay**, as in CQ's party unity votes. Absent and present members were
  counted as not voting Yea, so a party's absences could decide the label:
  a 143-73 Republican vote against the Democrats' 0-212 read bipartisan.
  8 of the 1,588 stored roll calls change label (6 Senate, 2 House), and
  with them the voted-with-party flag of the members who voted on them.

## Also in this release (no score effect)

- Partisan depth credits each policy area a bill touches with the bill's
  own lean, from the roll call where there is one, not with each area's
  content label (AGENTS.md §4).
- House sponsored bills show the CRS policy area Congress.gov assigns, as
  the Senate's do, and are classified on the title and that area together.
- A member who caucuses with neither party has the vote-part status
  "no party line", not "few votes".
- Leaderboard trend arrows compare only snapshots that recorded the same
  algorithm version.
- The donor-vote cards headed each industry's total with the industry's
  largest donor, which read as that one donor's money (one education
  total of $460K under a single university's name). The industry is now
  the heading and the largest donor is named on its own line.
- Vote dates are stored as ISO dates, so the voting record lists votes in
  calendar order.
