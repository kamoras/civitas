# Member score v6.25

## Legislative Effectiveness: coalition attraction is compared within the member's party

**Before (v6.24).** The bipartisan coalition component (15% of Legislative
Effectiveness) takes the share of the cosponsors a member attracts to their
own bills who come from the other party, and scores it against the
chamber's median member: the median scores 50, twice the median or more
scores 100.

**What went wrong.** A sponsor can only attract the cosponsors the other
party is willing to give, and the parties are not equally willing. On the
live 119th Congress breakdowns (2026-10-01) the Democratic median member
scored 29 (Senate) and 33 (House) on this component, against 62 and 67 for
Republicans. The other two components of Legislative Effectiveness were
already close to even by party, so this one component accounted for most
of the Republican lead (median 53 vs 49 in the Senate, 55 vs 50 in the
House).

The obvious suspect was majority status. The majority controls the agenda,
and minority members may avoid co-signing bills that won't move. If that
were the cause, the gap would flip under a Democratic majority. It doesn't.
On random samples of bills with at least one cosponsor (Congress.gov
cosponsor lists, 2026-10-01), the mean share of a bill's cosponsors from
the other party was:

| Congress (majority) | Chamber | Republican bills | Democratic bills |
|---|---|---|---|
| 117th (D) | House | 19.0% (n=152) | 15.8% (n=256) |
| 117th (D) | Senate | 35.3% (n=159) | 29.4% (n=260) |
| 119th (R) | House | 26.7% (n=274) | 25.6% (n=138) |
| 119th (R) | Senate | 38.7% (n=254) | 33.4% (n=170) |

Republican-sponsored bills draw the larger cross-party share under both
majorities, so the driver is party, not status: Democrats cosponsor across
the aisle more readily. A chamber-wide median therefore scored, in part,
the other party's cosponsoring habits rather than the member's own
coalition-building. The member-level gap is larger than the bill-level one
because a member's rate weights each bill by its cosponsor count, and
party-line bills with long cosponsor lists pull it down.

Harbridge-Yong, Volden & Wiseman (2023), the study this component rests on,
estimate the effect of attracting cross-party cosponsors with party and
majority status controlled. Pooling both parties against one median dropped
that control.

**Now.** The receive rate is scored against the median member of the
member's own party (`compute_bipartisanship_scores(direction="receive")`).
Everyone in a party faces the same pool of potential cosponsors from the
other party. A party with fewer than ten measured members falls back to the
chamber median. Normalizing by majority status was considered and rejected
on the table above. The give-and-receive blend shown on profiles keeps the
chamber median, the Lugar Index's convention: half of it is the member's own
choice to cosponsor across the aisle.

**Effect.** Recomputed from the 2026-10-01 live breakdowns. Members move by
1.8 (House) to 2.4 (Senate) points of Legislative Effectiveness on average,
and at most 6.1.

| Chamber | Party | Median LES, before → after | Mean LES, before → after |
|---|---|---|---|
| Senate | D | 49.0 → 52.7 | 53.2 → 56.2 |
| Senate | R | 53.0 → 51.5 | 54.8 → 53.0 |
| House | D | 50.0 → 51.8 | 51.0 → 52.4 |
| House | R | 55.0 → 51.6 | 56.4 → 54.2 |
