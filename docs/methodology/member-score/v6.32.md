# Member score v6.32

Two corrections found by the 2026-10-08 data audit.

## The candidate's own money is not outside money

Top-donor concentration, part of Funding Independence, is the top ten
outside donors' share of all outside money. Its stated definition takes the
candidate's own money out of that pool, but only the itemized donor rows
the classifier typed as self-funded were taken out. A candidate's loans to
the campaign and the candidate's own contributions, which the FEC reports as
separate totals, stayed in it as if a donor had given them.

Measured on the stored FEC totals: 95 sitting members have money from the
candidate in their election window; for 29 it is more than 10% of the
shares' base, and for one it is 95%. One campaign funded 85% by its
candidate's loans read its top ten donors as 1.3% of outside money.

Now the candidate's loans and contributions (FEC totals) are taken out of
the pool, and shown in the funding breakdown as their own category,
"Candidate's own money", instead of "Other sources". They stay in the base
the PAC and small-donor shares are taken over, as before: money a campaign
takes from its own candidate is money it didn't need from PACs. The category
is never an industry. These members' top-donor concentration rises and that
part of Funding Independence falls; the effect is measured on the first run
and added here.

## A vetoed bill is not a law

Congress.gov gives "Vetoed by President." the same action code (E30000) as
"Signed by President.", and the code alone decided the stage. Two bills
vetoed in December 2025, whose overrides failed, were stored as enacted:
they counted toward their sponsors' Legislative Effectiveness and were
listed under "Became law". The President action's own text now decides
signed or vetoed. Of the presidential action codes in the stored action
histories, E30000 was the only one that carried both (118 signings, 2
vetoes).
