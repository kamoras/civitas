# Member score v6.27

## Constituent Alignment: a roll-call position counts at the strength its votes support

**Before (v6.26).** Position congruence (30% of Constituent Alignment)
compares a member's congress-specific Nokken-Poole position with what a
same-party member of a seat with that lean typically holds. Every position
counted in full, however many roll calls it was estimated from. Voteview's
placeholder for a member it could not scale (0, 0) was read as a centrist
position.

**What went wrong.** A position estimated from a few roll calls is mostly
noise. Members sworn in late, members who left early, and everyone early in
a Congress were scored on such estimates at full strength.

**Now.**

- **Each position counts at the strength its votes support.**
  - The member's distance from the seat's expected position is scaled by
    how well a position from that many votes predicts a full record's,
    before it is read against the scale.
  - A position from 46 votes counts half, and a full record (200 or more
    votes) counts in full. The curve is weight(n) = min(1, w(n) / w(200)),
    w(n) = n / (n + n0).
- **How it is measured, on Voteview's own positions:**
  - The data are members with a thin record in one Congress and a full one
    in the next, or the reverse: 95 such pairs in Congresses 101–119, from
    the 50 states (delegates excluded, and members who switched parties
    during a Congress, whose partial records reflect the switch). Their thin
    records come from arriving or leaving mid-Congress and from long
    absences, as many of the score's do; everyone's early-Congress record,
    the score's most common thin record, isn't among them, and whether it
    behaves like these is untested.
  - The fit regresses the full position on the thin one, both read from
    the party's center.
  - It separates that from Congress-to-Congress drift, measured for each
    chamber and transition from 8,079 pairs of full records, in the
    pair's own direction (forward for a member who arrived, reverse for
    one who left).
  - Full records show no gradient in their count, so they count 1. Where
    a full record starts, 200 votes, is a convention: nothing chose it
    over a lower count.
  - One curve for both chambers: the latest era's, fitted on the
    transitions since the 110th Congress (a split by convention, roughly
    the middle of 101–119, kept there on reruns). It is chosen by
    predicting forward, the use the weight is put to: each transition's
    thin pairs predicted from a fit on the earlier transitions only.
    Over 82 members the latest era's curve predicts the next Congress
    better than one curve over every Congress by 0.066 (standard error
    0.034); a curve per chamber predicts worse (0.038 worse, 0.040). A
    more complex curve has to win by more than the standard error (a
    convention), and the calibration decides this again on every rerun.
  - The decision is weakly settled. Most of the gain is at two early
    transitions (the 112th and 114th); at the last three it is about
    one standard error. One member supplies 44% of it, and leaving the
    most-helped members out changes its size a lot (0.026 to 0.180)
    though never its sign. The era curve predicts better at every split
    from the 103rd to the 117th, but would be adopted at only 11 of 16,
    and a window of the last six transitions, with no split, does as
    well: the evidence is for recency, not a break at 2007. This rule
    was adopted in review, after a forward check had been run once and
    had shown the era curve winning (the third rule tried), so it is
    disclosed as such; the half points under the two rules, 46 and 36,
    each lie inside the other's interval.
  - Left out one member at a time (the earlier drafts' rule), the era
    structure's gain is all in the earlier era, whose n0 sits at the
    grid's floor on only 4 early thin pairs under 50 votes; on the latest
    era's members its curve does no better than one curve (0.0025 worse,
    standard error 0.0195): that comparison lets one curve learn from the
    Congresses it is judged on. Later splits and a time trend (one line,
    log n0 linear in decades since the 110th, added after the other
    tests, reported only) lean the same way as the forward test.
  - The half point is weakly determined: its 90% interval is 26–98
    votes. The upper end is where the n0 search stops: the data can't
    rule out a weight that simply grows with the count, n / 200.
  - `scripts/calibrate_position_confidence.py` writes it to
    `app/data/position_confidence.json`, reproducibly.
  - The split is a fixed Congress, so nothing in it follows the sitting
    Congress: a new Congress needs no rerun and no setting. A rerun only
    adds new pairs, to the latest era.
  - A party-line term (the curve's speed varying with the Congress's
    share of party-line roll calls) predicts held-out members worse. A
    curve by which side of the pair is thin (mostly arrived against
    left) and one by attendance (attended arrivals and departures
    against the rest) can't be applied to a sitting member's record, and
    neither predicts better than one curve by more than the noise.
- **Positions with no count.** Voteview sometimes publishes a position
  with no vote count, for a member it has barely scaled. Where the member
  has a career DW-NOMINATE position, such a position counts 0.21
  (0.01–0.39), measured the same way on 25 such pairs; leaving out one
  member at a time moves it between 0.13 and 0.26. Where they have
  none (just sworn in), it counts nothing, so the part sits at 50.
- **No cutoff for the fits; full records for the scale.**
  - The seat fits use every member, since a thin position is noisy, not
    biased.
  - The saturation scale is the 90th percentile of full records only.
    Early in a Congress, with fewer than 40 of them (a convention), the
    chamber's last scale is carried, so thin positions are pulled toward
    50 rather than rescaled back out. With every record at 5 votes, a
    position at saturation scores about 45 instead of 0.
- **Members without a position.** Voteview's placeholder is not a
  position and is left out. A member the current Congress's data has no
  position for counts nothing, so the part sits at a neutral 50. The
  component is no longer dropped for them, which had let the vote part
  carry their whole score. This applies from the first v6.27 Voteview
  ingest; a section written before it is read as before until then.
- **Current term only.** Each Voteview section records its Congress, and a
  position counts only for roll calls of the same Congress, or the sitting
  one for a member matched to no roll call (principle 6).
  - Once a new Congress's roll calls are being scored, the component is
    left out until that Congress's Voteview export passes the ingest
    gates, as when no data exists.
  - A stored score's "show the math" keeps reading the positions it was
    scored on, while that Congress's section is on disk, until the next
    run rescores it.
  - The weight applies to the DW-NOMINATE fallback too. That is an
    uncalibrated extension: the curve was measured on Nokken-Poole
    positions.
- **Flank breaks.**
  - The rule that decides whether a break goes toward the other party now
    weights the defectors' and the party's mean positions by reliability.
    On the 119th Congress's party-unity roll calls the weighting changes
    none of 2,351 Senate breaks and 2 of 6,055 House breaks.
  - The rule reads any Congress's positions, since it needs only which
    side of their party the defectors sit. Early in a new Congress, the
    last positions classify its breaks rather than every flank break
    counting.
  - A weighted mean can't help with a lone defector: their side is just
    their own position's, however thin. So once a new Congress's section
    is in, the rule reads a member's last-Congress full record, which the
    section keeps for this rule only (never scored), until their new
    record is full (200 votes). A member with no usable position this
    Congress is read on the last Congress's, however short (a stated
    choice, not measured); once the new section is in, a member who
    switched parties is never read on it, and a position recorded under
    the other major party is never read for the member at all (also a
    stated choice). Each Congress's positions are read from their own
    party's weighted mean.
  - The 200 is a convention: the rule as designed, and the one count at
    which the new record needs no model to be trusted. The calibration
    would adopt an earlier switch only if one, chosen on resampled
    members and judged by the model fitted to those left out, saved
    sides in 95% of resamples (also a convention, and strict). The test
    belongs on pairs shaped like the rule's case: a full record, then the
    first, attended votes of the next Congress from a member who left
    during it (absent the Congress after; attended by a convention:
    missing no more of their span than nine in ten full records). Most
    leavers missed more than that; only 3 pairs have the shape, too few
    to measure a switch. Counting every member absent the Congress after,
    attended or not (33 pairs), a switch saves sides in 3% of resamples;
    over all thin pairs, 36%. None is near the bar.
  - This first applies in the 120th Congress, since a 119th section
    written before v6.27 keeps no earlier positions.
- **Ingest fixes.**
  - The House's gate counted members with a position against a limit of
    450. By October 2026 the 119th House export had 451, counting
    delegates and mid-Congress replacements, so every House refresh had
    failed and kept older data. The gate now counts seats (380–435 House,
    45–50 Senate), plus a floor on seated members.
  - Exports that write party codes as floats ("200.0", as the 115th–117th
    do) failed to parse. They parse now.
  - A member who switched parties during a Congress is listed twice, under
    two ids, and was read on whichever row came last, with both rows
    entering the seat fits. They are now read on the record since the
    switch, and only it enters the fits: a choice (the record of who the
    member now is), which the evidence is consistent with but can't
    settle. Over 8 people with a full record in the next Congress, the
    record since the switch is nearer it than the longer record (by 0.050,
    standard error 0.032) or a vote-weighted mean (by 0.030, 0.022). Just
    after a switch that record is short and counts little; until Voteview
    places it, the member has no position; and, once the new section is
    in, the flank rule never reads their last Congress, cast in the old
    party. The record since the switch is the id the last Congress's
    export lacked, or, if both are new, the one whose first roll call
    comes last; if the exports needed for that can't be read or don't
    settle it (two new ids, neither yet voting), the ingest keeps the
    previous section and raises an ops alert. The flank rule also never
    reads a last record from another party when the party changed between
    Congresses (a new id in each; a section written before this change
    records no parties, so this starts with the next Congress's section).

**What was tested and kept.** The same study tested the two other ways this
component differs from the vote part. Neither changed:

- **50 at the expectation, not 100.** Across House general elections
  1994–2010 and Senate general elections 1990–2024, the linear shape
  predicted the incumbent's vote share (t=5.2 and t=2.5). Peaking it at the
  expectation, as the vote part does, predicted nothing (t=−0.4, t=−0.7).
  Sitting nearer the seat's center earns credit with voters, so it scores
  above 50. A typical member scoring about 50 here is expected.
- **One scale for both parties.** One scale per party predicts elections
  equally well, and the test of which unit voters respond to found no
  difference between the parties under either. The pooled scale stays.
  The pooled scale leaves the party averages about a point apart (over
  every Senate since 1989, unweighted, 50.5 D and 51.6 R pooled; 50.8 and
  50.9 with one scale per party). Republican positions spread more widely
  around their expectation, so more Republicans sit near the ends of the
  scale.

The study is `docs/research/constituent-alignment.md`, section 14
(reproduce with `scripts/research_constituent_alignment.py`). That section
also records the earlier drafts of this change and why they were replaced:
- A cutoff of 81 votes from a noise proxy, which resampling put anywhere
  from 11 to 157.
- A re-estimation from random subsets of votes, which understates how noisy
  a real run of consecutive votes is.
- A single drift for every Congress, which made the curve appear to depend
  on party-line voting and shrank every full record about 10%.
- One curve per chamber, shipped on a held-out margin within the noise;
  the one-standard-error rule now decides.
- Flank-rule switches at 53 and then 92 votes, chosen in sample, the
  first on pairs centered differently from the rule; neither can be shown
  to help: too few pairs have the rule's shape to measure a switch, and
  over all thin pairs the best (101) saves sides in only 36% of
  resamples.
- Thin pairs restricted to members who arrived or left, taken to make
  every thin record a run of consecutive votes; many are mostly absences,
  and the score's thin records include absences too, so the weight uses
  every thin pair.
- Party switchers counted as a member who left and one who arrived (half
  point 50). Four of 99 thin pairs were switches; without them one curve
  gives 36.
- Choosing the structure by leaving one member out: first over every
  member, which picked the era structure on a gain that was all in the
  earlier era; then on the latest era's members alone, which kept one
  curve (half point 36). Both let a curve learn from the Congresses it is
  judged on; predicting forward, the latest era's curve does better.

**Effect on the 119th Congress** (Voteview exports of 2026-10-03):

| | Senate | House |
|---|---|---|
| Saturation | 0.258 → 0.268 | 0.224 → 0.221 |
| Mean change in position congruence | 1.69 | 0.58 |
| Mean change in Constituent Alignment | 0.51 | 0.17 |

Full records move only through the scale and the refit seat lines. The
Senate's mean change comes from dropping its placeholder, which moves the
Republican fit, and from its scale now being measured on full records.
Thin records, and those with no count or no position:

- Two recently sworn-in representatives with no count and no career
  position move from 23.3 and 31.1 to 50 on the component.
- Representatives with 77 and 72 votes move from 93.2 to 79.7 and from
  75.3 to 66.7.
- A representative far beyond saturation (135 votes) stays at its end;
  the others with 116–171 votes move about 1 to 4 points, and senators
  with 53 and 187 votes by under 1.
- A representative who left their party during the Congress is read on
  their record since the switch, as before, but their old record no longer
  enters the seat fits. With no major party, the score reads them against
  the caucus the pipeline infers from their votes; they are left out of
  these figures.
- Members scored from 1 to 39 votes or a placeholder, all since departed,
  move by up to 50 points.

Separately, House positions refresh again now that the gate is fixed.
