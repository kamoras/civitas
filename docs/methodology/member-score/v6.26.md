# Member score v6.26

## Funding Independence: complete FEC detail, industries from occupations, and fairer comparisons

Funding Independence has four components: PAC dependency, small-donor
share, top-donor concentration and industry concentration. v6.26 changes
the data under the last two and how three of the four are compared. The
small-donor share is unchanged.

### 1. The detail behind the shares is complete, not sampled

**Before (v6.25).** Top donors and the industry breakdown were built from
Schedule A receipts the pipeline fetched page by page: committee
contributions plus a sample of itemized individual contributions grouped
by employer. The FEC API's rate limit (0.25 requests a second) kept that
sample to roughly a hundred donors per campaign. A Senate campaign raising
tens of millions in small gifts listed under fifty donors and was scored a
neutral 50 for top-donor concentration, "too few donors to measure",
although it needed almost no big donors at all.

**Now.**

- **Committee money** comes from the FEC's bulk contributions-by-committee
  file (`pas2{yy}.zip`, `fetch_committee_contributions`): every committee
  that gave to the member's authorized committees, totalled per committee.
  One file per cycle covers every member. For one large Senate campaign
  checked by hand, its total was $3.55M against the $3.44M the FEC reports
  for the same committees and cycles; the difference is refunds the file
  lists separately.
- **Individual money** comes from the FEC's own totals of itemized
  contributions by occupation and by employer (`fetch_occupation_totals`,
  `fetch_employer_totals`, the first `OCCUPATION_PAGES` pages, largest
  first). For the same campaign, the occupation totals covered $12.9M of
  money with a stated occupation, against $1.6M in the employer sample used
  before.

Both are cached for 30 days, since a completed election's totals do not
move.

### 2. Industries come from what donors say they do

**Before.** An employer's name went to the embedding classifier. It filed
"Computer Systems Design" under HEALTHCARE. Most individual money was never
placed in an industry at all: 0.28% of one grassroots senator's money was
classified in the 2026-07 audit.

**Now.** A donor's stated occupation counts toward an industry when most
people in that occupation work in it (`transform/occupation_industry.py`,
`app/data/occupation_industry.json.gz`, generated by
`scripts/fetch_occupation_industry.py`):

- The occupation-to-industry table is the Census Bureau's ACS microdata
  (PUMS, occupation by industry, person-weighted). An occupation is assigned
  an industry only when at least half of its workers are in that industry;
  "Registered nurse" is HEALTHCARE, "Manager" is not assigned.
- The industry side is a crosswalk from NAICS codes, the federal taxonomy
  the ACS uses, to Civitas's industries.
- A stated occupation is recognized only by an exact match with one of
  O*NET's job titles for the occupation, after normalization (case,
  punctuation, plurals). Matching unrecognized strings to their nearest
  title by embedding was tested and dropped: it was right about the
  industry 44% to 71% of the time, depending on the threshold.

Occupations that are not industries ("Retired", "Not employed",
"Homemaker") and occupations with no majority industry stay unattributed.
On the 2026-10-01 Senate data, this places a median 24% of a senator's
itemized individual money in an industry (11% to 39%, p10 to p90), and a
median 17% of all contributions (7% to 33%) once committee money is
included.

### 3. PAC dependency: the seat's expectation, not the campaign's size

**Before (v6.22).** A senator's PAC share was compared with a fit of PAC
share against campaign size, and the gap was taken in proportion. A 5% PAC
share in a large campaign scored 17, as 1.7 times the 3% the fit expected.

**What went wrong.** Campaign size is the share's own denominator. PAC
dollars barely vary with it (r = 0.17), so a fit of share on size has a
slope near −1 by construction: −0.98 in log-log on the 2026-09 Senate. That
turns "share of the campaign from PACs" back into "PAC dollars against the
typical senator's", the spurious-correlation problem of regressing a ratio
on its own denominator (Pearson 1897; Kronmal 1993).

**Now.** The expectation comes from the seat (`_pac_expectation`):

- **Senate:** the PAC share senators from a state of that size typically
  take, fitted on the chamber's senators each run (`pac_population_fit`,
  share against ln of state population). Small states have small donor
  pools, and their senators take more from PACs: r = −0.49 on 2026-10-01.
  Population is fixed by the seat; campaign size is not.
- **House:** the chamber median. Districts are apportioned equal
  populations. Seat safety was checked as a predictor and not used: the
  median PAC share runs from 27% in swing districts to 44% in safe ones, but
  the correlation with |PVI| is only +0.14.

The gap is now in share points, scaled by the chamber's own p10-to-p90
spread around the expectation, so a member at the expectation scores 50 and
one a full spread below scores 100.

### 4. Both concentration components are ranked within the chamber

**Before.** Each concentration was scored against the chamber's median and
spread. Both are shares bounded at zero and skewed right, so the best score
a member could reach was about 65, even with no big donors at all.

**Now.** Both are ranked (`_rank_score`): 100 minus the member's percentile
in the chamber, interpolated through its measured deciles. No concentration
scores 100, the median member 50.

- **Top-donor concentration** is the top ten donors' share of all outside
  contributions (the contribution base less the candidate's own money), the
  definition of a concentration ratio over the whole market. Until v6.26
  the denominator was the sampled donor list.
- **Industry concentration** is the HHI of the classified industry money's
  mix (Parmigiani 2025 computes the same HHI per legislator), needing at
  least $250,000 of classified money. It is no longer pulled toward 50 in
  proportion to the share of all funding classified: that read a campaign
  of small gifts as an unreliable estimate however much industry money it
  had.

An HHI over everything raised, as top-donor concentration is taken, was
built and measured, then rejected. Over everything raised, the HHI tracked
how much of a member's money the classifier could place (r = −0.91 with the
classified share), and per section 2 that is mostly how many donors' stated
occupations resolve, which varies from 11% to 39%. The mix of the money that
is placed estimates the composition whatever the coverage. How much of a
campaign is industry money is already the PAC and small-donor components'
question.

**Industry concentration is ranked within the member's party.** On the
2026-10-01 data, ranked across the chamber, it averaged 27.6 for Senate
Democrats and 69.7 for Republicans (House: 37.9 and 61.7). That gap is not
in what members do. Democratic donors' stated occupations fall in fewer,
broader categories (education, health care, law), Republican donors' in
more, finer ones (finance, real estate, energy, construction, agriculture),
and an HHI depends on how finely the categories are cut. As Legislative
Effectiveness's coalition component does (v6.25), each member is now ranked
against their own party's deciles (`industry_hhi_by_party`). A party with
fewer than 10 measured members, in practice an independent, is ranked
against the chamber. Ranked within party, the component averages 50.3 for
Democrats and 50.5 for Republicans in the Senate, 50.3 for both in the
House. The other three components' party differences are left as they are:
they follow differences in the money itself (Republican senators took a
mean 25% of contributions from PACs, Democrats 14%).

Funding Diversity (informational, not in the overall score) keeps its v6.5
industry measure (`_industry_mix_diversity`); it is not ranked.

### 5. Every reference is measured each run

The PAC population fit, the PAC-share spread and both concentrations'
deciles are computed by `compute_funding_reference` from the members each
run is about to score, as the other Funding Independence references are
(AGENTS.md §3a).

### 6. A component that can't be measured is left out, not counted as 50

Until v6.26 a concentration component with nothing to measure (no donor
list, too little industry-classified money, no chamber reference) counted
as a neutral 50 at its full weight. That pulled every such member toward
50 by a gap in Civitas's data: a campaign scoring 100 on everything
measured lost 12.3 points for the industry part alone (13/53 of 50) and
21.7 when neither concentration could be measured, and a campaign scoring
0 gained as much. On the
v6.26 simulation 3 senators and 67 representatives had too little
classified money for an industry mix, and 1 senator no donor list.

Now an unmeasurable component is listed as not measured, and Funding
Independence is weighed over the components that were (their weights
renormalized). PAC dependency and the small-donor share are measured for
every member with FEC funding.

### Cost

Classifying a few hundred employers and committees per member one name at a
time cost about 40 seconds a member on the Pi. The payment-processor check
and the industry classifier now encode each member's names in one batch
(`skip_entities_batch`, `primed_industry_lookups`), about 3 seconds a
member.

### Effect

Simulated with the v6.26 code over every current member's FEC data
(fetched 2026-10-01 and 2026-10-02), against the stored v6.25 scores. One
representative has no FEC funding on record and stays neutral.

| Chamber | Members | Mean FI, before → after | Median, before → after | Mean change (absolute) | Moved up / down |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Senate | 99 | 49.8 → 50.0 | 51.0 → 50.0 | 12.6 | 41 / 55 |
| House | 432 | 55.0 → 51.7 | 55.0 → 52.0 | 10.2 | 158 / 258 |

| Chamber | Party | Mean FI, before → after | Median, before → after |
|---|---|---|---|
| Senate | Democrats (45) | 54.2 → 56.0 | 56.0 → 58.0 |
| Senate | Republicans (52) | 45.4 → 44.2 | 44.5 → 44.5 |
| House | Democrats (213) | 55.6 → 53.0 | 55.0 → 54.0 |
| House | Republicans (218) | 54.4 → 50.3 | 55.0 → 49.0 |

Changes run from −19.0 to +23.0 points (p10 to p90) in the Senate and
−19.7 to +13.0 in the House, at most +50 and −32. The movers sort by how
concentrated their money is: the ten largest losses in each chamber score
an average 21 (Senate) and 15 (House) on top-donor concentration under
v6.26, and the ten largest gains 84 and 80. Ranked, the median member
scores 50 on each concentration component, where v6.25's median-and-spread
scale gave no one more than about 65.

How the components relate to the small-donor share, Senate: top-donor
concentration r = +0.48, PAC dependency +0.69, industry concentration
−0.26. The House shows the same pattern, weaker.
