# Scoring

Moved from the README. AGENTS.md states the rules; this page keeps the longer description and the measurements behind them.

The reasons each score version changed are in [member-score/](member-score/) and [weights.md](weights.md).

## Scoring

**What the site shows (2026-10).** No composite grade and no rank: each member page compares four measures (votes against the party, money from PACs, money from small donors, bills past committee) with the member's own caucus in the chamber, each with the figure the seat predicts, and an evaluative word from a fixed percentile rule (see "Where a member stands among peers" below and `/about/scores#peers`). The three-part score below is still computed each night, for the record and for public API v1's deprecated `overall` and `rank` (served through the v1 sunset, `schemas.API_V1_SUNSET`, and not after); its weights' closing record is in `docs/methodology/weights.md`.

### Non-partisan by construction

The scoring formulas deliberately avoid any ground truth that encodes partisan assumptions. Every dimension measures *structural behavior* against a neutral baseline, not agreement or disagreement with any policy position.

```
Overall = 0.33 × FundingIndependence
        + 0.33 × ConstituentAlignment
        + 0.34 × LegislativeEffectiveness
```

The weights live in `SCORE_WEIGHTS` (`backend/app/config_definitions.py`) and are served at `GET /api/config` — that dict, not this file, is authoritative. Two former dimensions are excluded from the weighted overall: **Promise Persistence** (removed in v6.0 — a live measurement found 0 of 100 senators reached even "medium" evidence confidence, so the dimension had collapsed to its neutral prior; still stored, but published as `null` since 2026-10, because with no promises to read its stored value is only the vote-participation term on a neutral base, 55 for 97 of 100 senators) and **Funding Diversity** (folded into Funding Independence in v6.5 after an audit found the two correlated at r=0.72 — the same underlying funding-profile signal measured twice).

The exact formulas are actively iterated (v1 onward, each change measured against real data; the current version is `ALGORITHM_VERSION` in `score_calculator.py` and the first entry of `frontend/src/lib/scoreVersions.ts`, which `tests/test_score_version_sync.py` keeps in step) and are documented in full — every component's weight, calibration source, and academic citation — in the module docstring of `backend/app/pipeline/analyze/score_calculator.py`, which is the source of truth for the current rule; why each version changed is in `docs/methodology/` (one decision record per version). Rather than duplicate formulas here that will drift out of sync as the algorithm evolves (as this section previously did), a summary:

- **Funding Independence**: PAC dependency (PAC share against what the seat predicts: senators by state population, representatives by the House median, both fitted every run, in share points; v6.26), state-relative small-donor share (read to two decimals since v6.36; a whole percent before, so a share under half a percent counted as none), the top ten donors' share of all outside money (the candidate's own loans and contributions, per the FEC totals, are not outside money; v6.32), and the HHI of the industry mix (folded in from the former Funding Diversity dimension), both ranked within the chamber, industry concentration within the member's party (v6.26: one party's donors work in fewer, broader industries, which a chamber-wide rank scored as the member's). Donors and industries come from the FEC's complete detail (every committee contribution from its bulk file, itemized individual money by occupation) since v6.26, where 100 sampled receipts had covered under 1% of a large campaign; since v6.31 the detail covers exactly the cycles of the FEC totals it sits beside, and committee money in the industry mix is capped at what the campaign reported receiving from committees. v6.13 removed outside spending and source breadth after testing both against FEC data: outside spending tracks race competitiveness, and breadth was the small-donor share counted again (see `docs/research/funding-independence.md`).
- **Constituent Alignment** (keyed `constituentAlignment`; it was `independentVoting` until 2026-09 — the dimension was rebuilt in v4.2): how a member's voting compares to what their *seat* elected them to do. Party-line voting in a safe seat that elected that platform scores as representation, not as a failure of independence — the delegate model of representation (Miller & Stokes 1963), not independence as an intrinsic virtue. A House seat's lean is the Cook PVI of the district the member was *elected on* — the sitting Congress's lines, pinned per Congress in `app/data/district_pvi_sources.json` (`fetch/district_pvi.py`), never the map the next election uses; members switch to a new Congress's lines — together with the scored windows, which read the same setting — at the first House pipeline run in a job that starts after noon ET on January 3, when that Congress takes office (the setting follows the clock, advanced at the start of each pipeline job, no restart — a `CURRENT_CONGRESS` in `.env` is ignored, and pins the windows and the lines only with `ARCHIVE_MODE=true`, for an archived re-run; a Congress with no pin stays on the newest pinned lines until the weekly check adopts one from Wikipedia's table by rule, `district_pvi.adopt_new_pins`; those are its lines when no state redrew). Each stored House score records the Congress whose lines it used, and its "show the math" breakdown is recomputed on those same lines. The elections pages show the lines of the Congress each election seats — for a cycle nobody has pinned yet, the newest pinned lines. A senator's seat lean is the state's Cook PVI, computed from official returns of the two newest presidential elections (today 2020 and 2024: the FEC's certified 2020 state totals and the MIT Election Lab's 1976-2024 Dataverse file for 2024; each cycle is read from the FEC's compilation once its results index links one, else from MEDSL's newest file, the two cross-checked state by state, and `data_refresh` reruns it monthly, so a new election's returns move the window with no edit; the per-state counts are stored beside the figures and a test recomputes every figure from them) with Cook's weighting (75% to the recent election) by `scripts/fetch_state_pvi.py`; since v6.33 it reproduces all 51 of Cook's published state figures, where the equal-weight mean before it was a point off in 18. The member's break rate is scored against the break rate same-party members show at the same seat lean, measured from the chamber every run (`compute_constituent_reference`; since v6.35 the per-party line bends at a swing seat only when the bend predicts the party's members better out of sample, by leave-one-out quasi-likelihood and more than one standard error — `select_opposed_kink` — where a fixed count of five opposed seats used to decide, and each fit records the comparison under `kink`); the member's Nokken-Poole roll-call position (Voteview) is scored against a seat-conditional per-party expectation (Canes-Wrone, Brady & Cogan 2002's district-relative extremity). Both apply the same way in safe and competitive seats, and position is scored symmetrically — v6.13 decided each of those choices by testing them against House re-election results. Since v6.27 a position counts at the strength its roll calls support, min(1, w(n) / w(200)) with w(n) = n / (n + n0): a full record (200 votes or more, a convention) counts in full, and n0 is measured on Voteview's own thin and full records with drift per transition (`scripts/calibrate_position_confidence.py`, `app/data/position_confidence.json`), one curve for both chambers over every Congress since 1989, chosen by predicting the next Congress with each transition's n0 fitted on the earlier ones only (its drift, shared by every structure, from its own full pairs) and keeping the simplest structure whose error is within one standard error of the best's (the one-standard-error rule, a convention, adopted in review after four other rules had been tried, two leaving one member out, then two forward): the latest era's curve (transitions since the 110th Congress, a split by convention) has lower error than one curve by about two standard errors of the paired difference (just under two), about one over the last three transitions alone (computed from `forward_test` and its `last_three`), and a window of the last six transitions about as much (just over two), but both are well within the best's own standard error, a curve per chamber predicts no better, and with the split or the width chosen from earlier Congresses alone each has lower error than one curve only within the noise; nothing in it follows the sitting Congress); the saturation scale comes from full records, and a member with no position in a current section sits at 50 (until a new Congress's positions pass the gates the part is left out); the same study kept the 50-at-the-norm shape and the scale pooled across both parties (the pooled scale a stated choice: the evidence decides neither way) (`docs/research/constituent-alignment.md`, section 14). The vote score is highest when a member breaks with their party about as often as same-party members of similarly-leaning seats, and falls both ways (v6.16): the gap is measured in standard deviations per vote (`seat_residual`, the Pearson residual against a fractional-logit expectation), so a few extra points count for more where members of that seat rarely break, and it reaches 0 at 1.5 of the party's 90th-percentile gaps above the expectation or 3 below, so loyalty costs half as much as breaking too often. Each party is read on its own scale, so a party that is more unified in a given Congress isn't scored higher for it. The member's own party's primary voters reward this shape; the general electorate rewards breaking more than the norm — the score follows the first, as a stated choice (see `docs/research/constituent-alignment.md`, sections 8–12). Ideal points are ingested automatically every pipeline run (`app/pipeline/fetch/voteview.py`, ingestion-gated) — no manual step.
- **Legislative Effectiveness**: Volden & Wiseman's (2014) Legislative Effectiveness Score — each bill counted at every stage it reaches, divided by the chamber's total at that stage, so advancing a bill counts for far more than introducing one (v6.14; `docs/research/les-stage-weighting.md` checks it against their published scores). Five stages, as in V&W: introduced, action in committee, action beyond committee, passed the chamber, became law (v6.17). A bill reported out of committee shows as "Reported by Committee" and one under floor debate as "On the Floor" (added 2026-09, when S. 4668 read "In Committee" through a week of Senate floor votes); both are credited as action beyond committee. `scripts/research_les_stage_classifier.py` checks the stage each bill is given against V&W's per-member counts for the 118th Congress — benchmarked against the median member of the sponsor's majority/minority status in their chamber, re-measured from its current members every pipeline run, cosponsorship-network leadership (PageRank, tenure-confidence-scaled), and bipartisan coalition attraction (v6.11, moved from Constituent Alignment — the receive-only share of cross-party cosponsors a member attracts to their own bills, the construct Harbridge-Yong, Volden & Wiseman 2023 show predicts lawmaking success; compared with the median member of the member's own party since v6.25, because Republican-sponsored bills draw more cross-party cosponsors under either majority).

### Senate and House scores

Each senator and House representative carries five sub-scores (0-100, higher = better); the three in `SCORE_WEIGHTS` are weighted into the overall, the other two are informational:

| Metric | Weight | What It Measures | Key Reference |
|--------|--------|------------------|---------------|
| **Funding Independence** | 33% | PAC dependency + small-donor share + top-donor concentration + industry concentration | Stratmann 2005; Parmigiani 2025 |
| **Constituent Alignment** | 33% | Break rate vs. same-party members in same-lean seats + roll-call position congruence (Nokken-Poole vs. seat-conditional norm); senators also: approval among the state's other-party voters and independents (CES), around the typical senator | Carson et al. 2010; Canes-Wrone, Brady & Cogan 2002; Nokken & Poole 2004; Ansolabehere & Kuriwaki 2022 |
| **Legislative Effectiveness** | 34% | Stage-normalized Volden & Wiseman LES (majority-status-benchmarked) + cosponsorship leadership (PageRank) + bipartisan coalition attraction | Volden & Wiseman 2014; Harbridge-Yong, Volden & Wiseman 2023 |
| Promise Persistence | unweighted (v6.0) | Published as `null`: campaign-promise tracking was removed in 2026-07, so its formula (commitments kept vs. broken + vote participation) has no promises to read, and the stored value is a near-constant | Naurin 2011; Martin 2011 |
| Funding Diversity | unweighted (v6.5, folded into FI) | Source breadth + industry diversity (inverse HHI) | Rhoades 1993; Parmigiani 2025 |

House representatives use the same scoring framework, data sources, and classification pipeline as senators, ensuring comparable scores across chambers (with chamber-specific references where the chambers' real baselines genuinely differ — the PAC-share fit, the effectiveness medians and spread, the break-rate expectation — each measured from that chamber's members every run, not typed in).

**Where a member stands among peers** (`app/peer_comparison.py`, served at `GET /api/{senators,representatives}/{id}/peers?group=caucus|chamber` and as `peerComparison` on the public breakdown). Four measures, each the member's plain figure from the facts the scorer stored beside the scores, in the order shown, which is how widely each matters: votes against the party (breaks on party-line votes), money from PACs (PAC share), money from small donors (share of contributions from donors who gave $200 or less in the cycle, the FEC's unitemized total) and bills past committee. Each answers one voter question (`question`: how does this member vote, who funds the campaign, do the member's bills move; rows sharing one sit under it once) and carries one fixed, neutral line saying what the figure is and why a voter might care (`why`, about grade 8) and a researcher's note (`measured`), all in `app/measures.py`; the "Stands out" line comes with `standsOutWhy`, the rule that picked it and that measure's why line. The why lines' sources are cited on `/about/scores#peers`. Each row carries the expected figure (the scorer's seat-adjusted reference where it has one: the state-size small-gift fit, the seat-lean break rate; else the peer median, and `expectedBasis` says which), every peer's figure, the member's mid-rank percentile, a template sentence, and a word by a fixed rule: "well above / below peers" outside the peer group's 10th to 90th percentile **and** more than one peer standard deviation from the expected figure, "above / below peers" outside the 25th to 75th, else "near peers". The cut points are conventions (CEL's exceeds / meets / below shape with quartiles and deciles), served with each comparison as `rule`; a row with under 20 peers or party-line votes, or whose dimension the scorer graded low, draws no word. Peers default to the member's caucus in the chamber (independents with the party they caucus with). The words are neutral about which direction is better: each measure states which way is higher. A measure whose unit is a count and whose figures are all whole numbers (bills past committee, a justice's opinions written) is drawn as a stacked dot histogram instead of a strip (`components/shared/CountHistogram.tsx`, 2026-10), since a strip piles everyone with the same count onto one point: one column per whole number, a dot per member stacked upward (several abreast in a wide column, with a dot's width between columns), the member or compare's pair ringed at the foot of their column, the median or expected figure dashed and labelled, and axis labels thinned to the width and text size. Stacks are capped at 200px, and a capped column says how many more it holds ("+39"); those members are read out at that label. Each dot is a readout mark (nearest dot under the pointer; the arrow keys walk everyone in value order, up each column then on to the next). It is decided by `unit` from the API, never a key, uses only fields every API version sends, and a response without `unit` keeps the strip.

Score history is tracked in `ScoreSnapshot` records. Since 2026-10-19 each member snapshot also keeps the four measures' figures that day (`facts`, filled from the member's stored breakdown when the snapshot is inserted, `models._snapshot_facts` with `app/measures.py`, so the pipeline's recorders and the analysis-code fingerprint are unchanged), served on both history endpoints; each "Where they stand" row draws its own measure over time from them. The composite score's trend line is no longer shown for members.

Additional member metrics, both chambers. Ideology and partisan depth are informational, not scored; the Leadership Score is also shown on its own, and is the leadership part of Legislative Effectiveness (25% of it, 30% when there is no coalition data):

| Metric | What It Measures | Technique |
|--------|------------------|-----------|
| **Leadership Score** | Legislative influence — how many peers cosponsor this member's bills | PageRank on cosponsorship graph (Brin & Page 1998) |
| **Ideology Score** | Behavioral ideological position derived from cosponsorship patterns | SVD on cosponsorship matrix (Tauberer 2012) |
| **Partisan Depth** | How deeply aligned with their party across policy areas | Yea/Nay ratio on D- vs R-leaning bills per area, with SVD ideology as a prior while votes are few; the label is the member's tercile within their own party |

### Supreme Court justices (not scored)

No justice is scored or ranked (justice v3, see [Phase 5](../data-pipeline.md#phase-5-justices)). Justice v2's score, independence from the appointing president, failed a placebo test: a window of the same length placed later in each justice's career reproduces most of the differences between justices. Two alternatives (a same-case baseline, a career-tenure curve) failed the same test. Each justice's appointer estimate is shown with its 95% confidence interval as information, and the Court-level finding is stated on the leaderboard and About pages: about 2.5 points once career timing is set aside, 95% interval −0.7 to +5.6. `docs/research/justice-scores.md` has the full study, including why justice v2 had replaced consistency and independence from the appointing party's bloc (both ranked justices by distance from the Court's median, Spearman −0.82 and −0.75).

### Presidential scores

What the site shows (2026-10): no 0-100 score for a president, overall or by part, and no ranking by one. A president's page leads with the historians' own rank (C-SPAN's Presidential Historians Survey, by total points, competition rank among rated presidencies: `historiansRank` / `historiansRated`, and the edition it is from, `surveyYear` / `surveyUrl` (`cspan_historians_survey.stored_edition`), on the breakdown's `historicalLegacy.facts`, `president_service.get_president_score_breakdown`), attributed to them and dated, then "Where they stand among presidents": each figure the presidency has (average approval and its change, an election margin before polling, jobs, growth against peer economies or before 1947, unemployment, inflation, the historians' points) as a strip among every other presidency with that figure, within the era its series covers, with n, a tick for the median or for what presidents starting from the same level saw, and a word from the members' percentile rule (no word under ten other presidencies or for a term under way). A presidency shorter than a full term (the sitting one, or one cut short) has its average approval and approval change set among every other presidency's figure over the same number of days from its first poll, rank, percentile, word, sentence and dots alike (`president_scorer.window_reference` keeps each predecessor's window figures by id as `values`; a presidency polled for fewer days is left out), since approval falls as a term goes on: until 2026-10-23 the row ranked the sitting president's 633 days against predecessors' full terms. A reference written before `values` existed falls back to full terms until the next president run. The approval row also gives each presidency's lowest and highest single poll with its month (`aside` for this presidency, "so far" for a term under way, and each dot's hover detail in `distributionDetails`), over the same polls its average covers, rows UCSB marks interpolated left out, and says the strip ranks averages, not single polls: "lower" was read as a lower single reading. Since Gallup stopped in February 2026 UCSB's series carries other pollsters, so the wording is "single poll", not "Gallup". `GET /api/presidents/{id}/peers` (`app/president_peers.py`, outside `pipeline/` so the fingerprint doesn't move) computes every figure, word and sentence; the page only places them. Below the strips, "Through the term" charts the term against the other presidencies (`GET /api/presidents/{id}/charts`, `app/president_charts.py`, `PresidentCharts.tsx`): every presidential election's electoral share (the National Archives' Electoral College results, `pipeline/fetch/electoral_college.py`, each election credited to the presidency in office a week after the next inauguration day, so 1824 goes to the House's choice and is marked decided by the House) and popular margin (the American Presidency Project's mandates table, from 1828), this presidency's elections solid and a president who succeeded without winning one said in words, not drawn as zero; approval by month of the term (every poll, from 1945), jobs added since the inauguration year (BLS payrolls via FRED's PAYEMS, annual averages from 1939; the president pipeline stores the series, unscored), and real growth in each calendar year of the term (MeasuringWorth) beside the middle half of other presidencies' years in the same era. Each chart has its caption, window, source and n inside the shared image's area and a table in words for screen readers; the middle half is drawn only where at least five other presidencies reach that point. The wording says what happened during the term, never why. The scores below are still computed, and served by public API v1 marked deprecated (served through the v1 sunset, `schemas.API_V1_SUNSET`, and not after); the old president leaderboard redirects to the presidents' directory, and `/compare/presidents` sets the records side by side with the historians' rank.

Presidents are scored on three dimensions — Public Mandate (25%), Effectiveness (25%) and Historical Legacy (50%), each president's shown as the share it actually carries when a dimension is missing — using a mix of live data (BLS payroll jobs, FRED unemployment and consumer prices, MeasuringWorth real GDP, Maddison/World Bank peer-economy GDP, Voteview House polarization) and historical records (C-SPAN Historians Survey, UCSB American Presidency Project approval and election margins). Independence, Follow-Through, and Competence were removed in 2026-07 rather than left as hand-set values with no live formula behind them; a president with no data source for a dimension shows N/A and the overall score renormalizes over whichever dimensions actually apply. See the [scoring changelog](https://civitas-research.org/changelog) for the full account.

A member's dimension falls back to a neutral 50 when its data is missing or too thin to trust (a new member, a source with nothing for them); a part resting on few observations is shrunk toward a reference (Constituent Alignment's voting part toward its party's typical score, its position part and Legislative Effectiveness's leadership part toward 50), while Funding Independence drops a part it can't measure and Legislative Effectiveness's bill part is not shrunk; a president's dimension with no data source shows N/A instead. A record that is present but empty is not missing: a member with no substantive bills after half a year in office scores Legislative Effectiveness on a credit of 0. An outage is not a record either: when Congress.gov can't give a run a member's sponsored legislation, the member is scored on the bills an earlier run stored, and the breakdown names that run's date; with none stored, the bill part is not measured and leadership and coalition attraction carry its weight. A bill whose action history can't be read is staged from its latest action and degrades only itself, and the run's ops alert names it. No LLM input is used in score calculation — formulas are deterministic and auditable.

## Score data transparency

A senator's or representative's profile (`components/scorecard/MemberScorecard.tsx`) shows no composite score or rank (redesign 2026-10): a header with who they are, committees as chips, and three contact buttons (call, write naming the contact form's host, website); then "Where they stand" (`components/scorecard/WhereTheyStand.tsx`, data from `GET /api/{senators,representatives}/{id}/peers`, see Scoring): one row per measure (small gifts, PAC money, breaks with party, bills moved), the figure, a strip with every peer as a dot, the member as a ringed marker and the expected figure as a dashed tick, the backend's word and template sentence, a "Stands out" line above, a radio switch between the caucus and the whole chamber (kept in the URL as `?peers=chamber` with `history.replaceState`), and each row's peer values as a table (screen readers always; everyone after "Show as a table"); then "The record behind it": money, votes and bills side by side, each led by one plain sentence of what the record shows, then the evidence behind it (no 0-100 score, component bars or weights: those stay only in the deprecated public API fields); then their financial-disclosure holdings as a pie beside the list; then tiles for what is on record but not scored (stock trades, donor-vote links, positions by policy area). The money column also links straight to the donor-vote links ("See how this money lines up with votes"), which on a phone otherwise sit about seven screens below it. Every full list — every vote, every donor, every bill — opens in a drawer over the page; each vote there is one line (what was voted on, linked to the bill's page, the question, the date, the member's vote, and whether it went against the party). On a phone the columns stack. At the foot of the profile, one link from the header ("methods, data & citation", `#methods`, which opens it), is the expert layer (`MethodsLedger.tsx`): for each column its sources, the period it covers, the record behind it (the count from the breakdown's facts, and `representationScore.confidence` as full, partial or thin: a volume-count grade), the seat lean for votes, the method's section of /about/scores and its citations, and a note when records changed after the nightly run; then the record as JSON from the public API (profile, votes, history), and an APA-style reference dated by the latest scoring run (`scoredOn`: the newest score snapshot's date and method version).

Every dot-per-person strip (a member's peers, `/congress/members/measures`, compare's `PairStrips`, a president's and a justice's strips, the Court's Martin-Quinn line) goes through one component, `components/shared/Strip.tsx` (2026-10). Pointing near the strip shows the nearest dot's label: name, party and seat ("D-CA", "R-TX-5") or a presidency's years, the figure as printed and its place on the row ("3rd highest of 15", "Tied for lowest of 433", "2nd from the right of 9" for a Martin-Quinn position). A click opens that page; on touch a tap shows the label and a tap on the label opens it. The label stays while the pointer moves onto it and closes on Escape (WCAG 1.4.13). Each strip is one Tab stop (`role="group"`): arrow keys move by value, PageUp and PageDown by ten, Home and End to the ends, Enter opens the page, and a polite live region reads the label; the screen-reader tables stay. Every word in the label is the API's: the place comes from `app/ordinals.rank_shown` (competition ranking, ties share a place), sent as `distributionRanks` beside `distributionShown` and `distributionSeats` on the member peer rows, `rank` on `chamber_measures` points, `distributionRanks`, `distributionYears` and `rank` on a president's rows (`years` for the presidency itself), `rank` on each justice's peer point and on each placed justice on `/api/court`. The place is a position on one figure's row for the label, never an order the page sorts by. Under each strip the tick's label ("median 2.13 million a year") sits on its own line at the tick, slid along so it stays inside the strip, with the axis's two ends on the next line (`StripAxis`); a row's big figure keeps its number large and puts any words after it ("million a year", "of 493") on a smaller line (`BigFigure`).

Each column's sentence and parts come from `GET /api/{senators|representatives}/{id}/score-breakdown`, which the profile page fetches with the profile. It serves the breakdown the run that stored the scores worked out with the scorer's own functions (`score_calculator.explain_scores`, kept with the scores in `score_breakdown`), so its components add up to the stored score even after the hourly bill refresh moves a bill (the page shows the sentence and facts, not the score); the scores are also recomputed on today's records, and the methods ledger says when records changed after the run ("1 bill changed after the run of ...", `sinceScored`). It returns every component (value, weight, and the scorer's own sentence on how it came about) plus `facts`: the numbers the scorecard's sentences state — PAC and small-donor shares and the comparison they are measured against, party-line votes and breaks and the seat's expected break rate, bills and joint resolutions by the furthest stage each reached and the simple and concurrent resolutions beside them (counted apart, as the component's own sentence counts bills, so a resolution the chamber agreed to never reads as a bill that passed). Each member dimension also carries `finding`: the column's first line, one sentence filled from those same `facts` by a fixed template (`services/score_findings.py`) — "Breaks with the party about twice as often as Democrats in states that lean the same way", "Gifts of $200 or less made up 44% of the money, more than the 26% typical for a state this size", "Of 54 bills sponsored this Congress, 1 became law, and 3 more passed the Senate". It names no one and uses no pronoun, and its words are held to about an eighth-grade reading level (Flesch-Kincaid: over 90 sampled members on 2026-10-11, a median of 5.3 and 97% at 8 or under). It lives outside `pipeline/` so rewording it never moves the analysis-code fingerprint. The page formats them and computes none. Every share a page states (the finding, the scorecard's own line through `facts.shown`, the peer strips and compare, and a race's money strip) is worded by one rule, `score_display.shown_share`: a whole percent rounded half up, and "under 1%" for a share above zero that would round to 0% (2026-10; until then the small-donor share was stored as a whole percent before that rule ran, so 25 members' sub-0.5% shares read "0%" of the money, and the race strip apportioned its percents to sum to 100, which can read one point off the same share elsewhere). A race's strip is that campaign's own FEC totals, so for a sitting member it can differ from the profile's figure, which is their most recent completed election: the strip says "this campaign". The header's score trend states the change `GET /api/{senators|representatives|presidents}/{id}/history` serves as `change`: since the earliest snapshot on the latest one's scoring method (and, for a member, Congress), never across a methodology change. The full voting record can be searched: `?q=` on `GET /api/{senators|representatives}/{id}/votes` matches the bill number in any spelling ("HR 123", "h.r.123", the number alone; a prefix with dots and spaces dropped, so "S 1" never finds S. Res. 15), or every word of the search in the bill's title, the vote's description, or its roll call's question and title, matched as the bills list matches (stemmed, as word starts, in any order), and the list's counts follow the search (`_scorecard_common.vote_search`; it reads the member's own indexed rows and their roll calls once, and matches the words in memory). Finding one vote among 193 meant paging through 13 pages of 15. `?period=` narrows the same list to a date range the server works out (`month`: the last 30 days on the Capitol's calendar, not the calendar month, which is empty for its first days and all through a recess; `congress`: from Jan 3 of the Congress in office; `all`), shown as LAST 30 DAYS / THIS CONGRESS / ANY DATE beside the search (`_scorecard_common.vote_period_start`). The full voting record's party-line figures (`votingRecord.votedWithPartyCount`, `votedAgainstPartyCount`, `partyLoyaltyPct`) are the counts Constituent Alignment scores (`score_calculator.party_line_tally`), so they agree with the column. Nothing in it is written by a model, and because it is the same code the pipeline scores with, the scorecard can't drift from its numbers. A president's profile (`components/scorecard/PresidentScorecard.tsx`) has no score: the historians' own rank, named and dated (the header's comparison link opens `/compare/presidents` with this president already chosen: a server-rendered page whose two native selects submit as a plain GET form, setting the historians' rank and the breakdown's `facts` side by side; `/compare` pairs members of Congress), then the strips among presidents (`PresidentStanding.tsx`, from `GET /api/presidents/{id}/peers`), each with its sentence, n, source and an accessible table, then stock trades, recent approval, the same person's other presidency and executive orders. A former president's page carries no "former" banner: the years served say it. A justice's page is not a scorecard (`components/justice/JusticeProfile.tsx`): it names the appointing president with the year the justice joined the Court, linked to that president's page, then draws each figure beside the same figure for every sitting justice (`JusticeFigures.tsx`: one dot per justice, this justice ringed, the median dashed, a caption sentence and a screen-reader table): the share of cases in the majority, the majority share of cases decided by one vote, opinions for the Court, dissents and concurrences written, years on the Court and the latest Martin-Quinn position. Then agreement with each colleague (served named and ordered, `agreement`: `[{id, name, share}]`, each name linked), the appointer estimate drawn as every sitting justice's 95% interval with a one-line caveat (`AppointerIntervals.tsx`), and every case. The figures, medians, peer positions and sentences come with the profile (`scorecard.figures`, `services/justice_figures.py`, outside `pipeline/`); the page computes none. A president's page lists every Supreme Court appointment that president made (`JusticesAppointed.tsx`, `GET /api/justices/appointed-by/{president_id}`): the justice pipeline stores the Federal Judicial Center directory's appointments since 1789 (`justice_appointments`, `store_court_seats`), each given to the president in office on its nomination date from the presidents table (so a president with two nonconsecutive terms gets each appointment on the right presidency), with the seat and years served. A justice with a page on the site links to it; any other is named with the years. An outage keeps the stored list.

The profile also lists the records the dimensions read:

| Score | Records shown |
|-------|---------------|
| Funding Independence | Top donors by amount with type and industry, PAC share, small-donor share, industry breakdown (`IndustryDonation`) |
| Constituent Alignment | Every counted break this Congress with the chamber's own per-party tally (served in the breakdown's `facts.breakVotes`), breaks from the party's flank listed apart (`facts.flankBreakVotes`), and the seat's expected break rate |
| Legislative Effectiveness | Sponsored bills and joint resolutions by the furthest stage each reached (simple and concurrent resolutions counted apart), with commemorative bills marked |

The layer surfaces the underlying `KeyVote`, `Donor`, `IndustryDonation` and `SponsoredBill` records rather than LLM-written explanations — the numbers are auditable against the source data.

## Academic grounding

Key algorithmic decisions and their academic backing:

| Decision | Rationale | Reference |
|----------|-----------|-----------|
| Embeddings over keywords for classification | Semantic similarity generalizes to unseen text; keywords are brittle | Reimers & Gurevych 2019 (Sentence-BERT) |
| kNN over LLM for donor classification | 5s vs 40min, no hallucinated categories, deterministic | Cover & Hart 1967; Snell et al. 2017 |
| Party alignment from the roll call's actual split only | "Broke with party" is defined by how the parties voted; content stands in only where no roll call recorded each party's vote | Poole & Rosenthal 1985; Laver et al. 2003 |
| Learning store as experience replay | Past classifications bootstrap future accuracy | Lin 1992; Yarowsky 1995 |
| Inverse HHI for funding diversity | Standard concentration metric from IO economics | Rhoades 1993 |
| Linear shrinkage toward 50 on thin evidence (fixed rate `min(n/k, 1)`, not empirical Bayes; Constituent Alignment's position part instead uses a measured reliability weight relative to a full record, v6.27) | Keeps a few votes or cases from producing an extreme score | Stein-type shrinkage idea: Efron & Morris 1975 |
| Votes with the president's stated position, beside the rate expected for the seat (informational) | A raw support rate mostly measures party and seat | Papke & Wooldridge 1996; Wilson 1927; Agresti & Coull 1998 |
| Seat-relative break rate for Constituent Alignment (expected rate by party and Cook PVI, fitted from the chamber each run) | Raw party-break rates mislead without constituency context | Carson et al. 2010; Papke & Wooldridge 1996 |
| Donation-vote correlation ≠ causation | Methodological caution in interpreting funding influence | Ansolabehere et al. 2003 |
| Fuzzy name matching for self-funded detection | SequenceMatcher handles spelling variations, middle names | Ratcliff & Obershelp 1988 |
| PageRank for legislative leadership | Cosponsorship network centrality measures peer influence | Brin & Page 1998; Tauberer 2012 |
| SVD ideology as a prior for partisan depth (linear blend) | Behavioral ideological signal regularizes sparse vote data | Poole & Rosenthal 1985; Efron & Morris 1975 |

See the [Methodology page](https://civitas-research.org/about) for full details and inline citations.
